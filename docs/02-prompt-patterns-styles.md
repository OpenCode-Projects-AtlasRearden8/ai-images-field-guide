# Prompt Patterns for Styles

A prompt isn't a wish, it's a *description*. The tool can't read your mind, but it reads your adjectives very well. This chapter gives you a pattern you can fill in, plus before/after examples you can copy and adapt.

## The base pattern

Fill in the brackets. You don't need every slot every time, but the more slots you fill, the fewer silent assumptions the tool makes.

**[medium] of [subject] [doing what] in [place], [lighting], [framing], [mood/style], [extras]**

- **Medium**: photo, 3D render, watercolor, pencil sketch, flat vector sticker, cross-stitch chart...
- **Subject**: be concrete. "A red 1969 pickup truck," not "a classic vehicle."
- **Place**: "in a hospital hallway at night" — place does huge work.
- **Lighting**: golden hour, overcast, single candle, bright studio, neon signs.
- **Framing**: close-up, wide shot, top-down, from the side, over-the-shoulder.
- **Mood/style**: cozy, grim, playful, 1970s magazine ad, children's book illustration.
- **Extras**: aspect ratio, "no people," color notes, camera-ish terms ("shallow depth of field").

:::card
**Before**

> `a picture of a sunset`

**After**

> `a wide photo of a calm lake at dusk, low golden sun, orange and pink sky reflected in still water, distant treeline silhouette, soft pastel palette, no people`

Same subject. The second one reads like a plan instead of a shrug.
:::

## Pattern 1: Add light before you add drama

Lighting words are the single biggest quality lever, and they're cheap to type. Try adding *exactly one* lighting phrase first.

:::card
**Before**

> `old barn in a field`

**After**

> `old red barn in a wheat field, foggy early morning, soft gray light, no people`

Notice we didn't ask for "beautiful" or "high quality." The fog and the soft gray light *are* the beauty; quality words just shove the dice around.
:::

## Pattern 2: Framing is a second camera operator

If the picture feels wrong but you can't say why, it's usually framing. Say what the camera is doing: "top-down view," "shot from knee height," "wide shot with the house small near the horizon."

:::card
**Before**

> `coffee cup on a table`

**After**

> `close-up of a cream ceramic coffee cup on a weathered oak table, top-down view, morning window light, steam rising, shallow depth of field`

Six spec changes turned an object into a *scene*.
:::

## Pattern 3: Name the medium and era, not just the look

"Painting of a ship" gets a lottery. "1800s oil painting of a three-masted ship, stormy sea, dark dramatic clouds, in the style of classic maritime portraits" gets a decision. Style words stack: era + medium + mood is a strong triple.

:::card
**Before**

> `a dragon, oil painting, fantasy art`

**After**

> `a red dragon perched on a mountain ledge, oil painting, dramatic storm clouds behind it, warm sunrise light catching its scales, wide cinematic framing, no text`

Era/medium first, then subject, then scene, then light. Same words, sorted.
:::

## Pattern 4: Negative prompts (when your tool has them)

Many tools let you describe what you *don't* want. Use them for the predictable failures: `blurry, watermark, text, extra fingers, distorted face`. They're a brake, not a steering wheel — a negative prompt can't fix a vague main prompt.

## Pattern 5: Aspect ratio is composition

Wide shots want wide canvases (16:9, 2:1). A landscape on a portrait canvas fights you the whole way, then you crop it and lose the composition anyway. Set the ratio before you generate.

## The style bank (borrow freely)

Keep a list of phrase triples that work for you. Steal mine to start:

| Want | Paste this style phrase |
|---|---|
| Cozy children's book | `children's book illustration, soft watercolor, gentle rounded shapes, warm light` |
| Clean modern | `flat vector illustration, limited palette of 3 colors, generous white space` |
| Moody photo | `overcast day, muted colors, slight fog, documentary photo style` |
| Retro poster | `1970s screen-print travel poster, warm oranges and teals, sunset gradient` |
| Pencil warmth | `graphite pencil sketch, visible paper texture, cross-hatch shading, white background` |

> Practice loop: pick any subject, make one image with just the subject, then re-roll with one pattern added. Compare. That comparison is the whole skill, and 10 minutes of it teaches more than an hour of tutorials.
