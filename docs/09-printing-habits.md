# Good Printing Habits

You made something you love. Now it's going on paper — a card, a poster, a book cover, a fridge. Printing is where digital images meet physical reality, and reality has rules: inches, dots, margins, ink. Here's the whole routine.

## The one number to know: 300 DPI

Print quality is measured in dots per inch (DPI). Paper wants about **300 pixels per inch** of final print size. The math:

| Print size | Pixels needed (at 300 DPI) |
|---|---|
| 4×6 in | 1200 × 1800 |
| 5×7 in | 1500 × 2100 |
| 8×10 in | 2400 × 3000 |
| 11×14 in | 3300 × 4200 |
| 11×17 in | 3300 × 5100 |

Check your image's pixel dimensions first; if it's short, upscale (Chapter 4) before printing, then look at it at 100% zoom. At 300 DPI, one inch on paper = 300 pixels on your screen; arm yourself with that when judging detail.

## Margins and bleed

- **Margins:** home printers physically can't print to the paper's edge — usually 3–6 mm (0.12–0.25 in) is off-limits. Design with a safe border so nothing important gets clipped.
- **Bleed:** commercial print shops want images that extend ~3 mm (0.125 in) *past* the trim line, so cutting never leaves a white sliver. Your print shop's template tells you exact numbers.
- **Full-bleed at home** needs borderless-printable paper and printer settings; check your printer's manual instead of fighting it.

## Screen colors vs. ink colors

Your screen makes color with light (RGB); ink is pigment (CMYK). They can't fully match — neon pinks and electric blues especially. Practical habits:

- Don't chase exact color matches on the first print.
- Prefer slightly muted, higher-contrast designs; they translate to ink better.
- For anything color-critical (a brand logo), ask your print shop about a proof.

## The test print ritual

1. **Print at 25–50% scale on plain paper first.** Cheap, fast, catches disasters.
2. **Look at it at arm's length, then across the room.** That's how anyone else will see it.
3. **Check zoomed details:** text sharpness, faces, hands (still hands — see Chapter 5), edges.
4. **Fix and repeat once.** Then print final on good stock.

Fifteen cents of test paper has saved me more than once from thirty dollars of photo stock and an afternoon.

## Choosing paper

- **Card stock (65–110 lb / 176–300 gsm):** cards, covers, anything that stands up.
- **Photo paper:** glossy for punchy color, matte for softer, less-glare looks. Matte is more forgiving of slight softness.
- **Standard copy paper:** fine for proofs; too flimsy for final cards.
- Match your printer to the paper type in the print dialog — the setting changes how much ink is laid down.

## Filing the wins

When a print succeeds: save the final file name with size and paper (`sunset-lake-print_8x10_matte.png`), save the prompt that made it, and keep a note of the paper that worked. Your second printing project will take a quarter of the time — that's the compounding the whole guide keeps promising.

> Bottom line: pixels before paper (300 DPI), margins and bleed by the numbers, always a test print, and write down what worked. Your future self, holding a good print, says thanks.
