# Copyright and Watermark Cautions

This chapter is education, not legal advice. Laws differ by country and change over time. I'll give you the lay of the land and point you at the places to check. When money or reputation is involved, that's the moment to check the actual rules (or ask a professional).

## Who owns what you generate? Check the tool's terms.

Here's the honest answer: **it depends on the tool, the plan you're on, and where you live.**

- Many popular tools grant you broad rights to use what you make, including commercially, *as long as you follow their terms* (some restrict by paid/free tier).
- Some tools insert their own watermark or label on outputs unless you pay.
- In the U.S., the copyright office has taken the position that **purely AI-generated images without meaningful human authorship can't be copyrighted by the person who typed the prompt.** Practical upshot: things you generate may be *usable by you* but might not be *protectable by you*.

**Your action:** before generating for anything commercial — a book cover, product listings, client work — read the tool's terms of service. Search inside them for: "commercial use," "ownership," "license," "watermark." Fifteen minutes now beats a takedown later.

## Don't remove other people's watermarks

If an image (AI or otherwise) carries someone's watermark or stock-agency label, that image is not yours to reuse — **and cropping the watermark off doesn't change that.** A visible watermark is a fence, not an obstacle course. Don't buy it? Don't take it.

## Don't watermark other people's work as yours — or fake watermarks at all

Two sides of the same coin: putting your watermark on an image you didn't make, or manufacturing a fake stock-agency mark to make an image *look* legitimately licensed, both misrepresent where work came from. It's the kind of thing that ends accounts and lawsuits.

## Styles vs. specific artists

It's one thing to generate "in the style of watercolor children's books." It's another to prompt with a specific living artist's name hoping to clone their look and undersell them with it. The second one is increasingly seen (including by courts, juries, and platforms) as riding on someone's name. More and more artists also ask tools not to be style-imitated at all; some platforms have opted-out lists. **Friendly rule:** name styles, eras, and techniques; be very careful naming living artists.

## Uploading other people's work

Two common beginner moves to avoid:

- **"Just to see the style":** uploading an artist's image to img2img or "describe this image" tools can violate the artist's rights *and* your tool's terms. If you need a style reference, describe it in words.
- **Client work:** if a client or friend sends you photos to edit, you need *their* permission to run them through AI tools — same respect rule as Chapter 8.

:::tip
Keep a tiny paper trail for anything you sell: the tool used, the date, your prompt, the terms-of-service page you checked. If anyone ever asks, you can answer honestly in one screenshot.
:::

## If you get it wrong

Everyone makes a first-week mistake (mine was a cropped watermark — before I knew better). The fix is the same every time: take it down, apologize if a real person was affected, and don't do it again. Most situations are resolved by exactly that.

> Where to look deeper: your tool's current terms of service, your country's copyright office website, and — if it's genuinely high-stakes — a lawyer. I'm a guidebook, not one.
